# Voice: base rules

These rules apply to everything you write: code comments, commit messages,
specs, docs, essays, memos. Write to be understood, not to impress.

Every other voice guide layers on these and none of them repeats them, so read
this file first and keep it in mind while reading the others. Where another
guide needs one of these rules, it cites this file.

## Words

- **Use the plain word.** "Use" not "utilize", "before" not "prior to". Take
  the everyday word over the technical one where both carry the same meaning,
  and keep the technical term where it is the precise one.
- **Prefer the concrete to the abstract, and a verb to an abstract noun.**
  "Decide", not "make a decision". An abstract noun hides who does what.
- **Cut.** Drop any word the sentence survives without. Cut intensifiers (very,
  deeply, fundamentally, simply, genuinely, profoundly) and words that assert
  weight without supplying it (real, actual, true, significant, substantial,
  considerable, critical, key). Name the thing instead.
- **No dead metaphors.** "Low-hanging fruit", "toe the line". A metaphor earns
  its place only where it is the shortest accurate description.
- **Be precise.** A vague word usually hides a decision you have not made.
  Name the specific thing. This matters most in specs, tickets and commit
  messages, where a reader acts on the word.

## Sentences

- **One idea per sentence.** Split any sentence that needs a second read.
- **Actor as subject, action as verb, active voice.** Say who does what. Use
  the passive only where the actor is unknown or beside the point.
- **End on the point, not on a cadence.** The words that carry the point go
  where they land, which is usually last.

## Readability

Readability is what the reader spends to get the meaning. These rules lower
it without lowering precision.

- **Open with what the reader already has, and close with what is new.** A
  paragraph reads as a chain, each sentence picking up where the last left
  off, rather than a row of separate facts.
- **Gloss a term once, at first use, and keep the term.** A precise term with
  a plain gloss beside it serves both the reader who needs the term and the
  reader who does not. A vague substitute serves neither.
- **Chunk.** Short paragraphs, one idea each. Headers are plain labels, not
  teasers. A list is for real enumeration, never for breaking up an argument.
- **Treat counts as diagnostics, not targets.** A sentence over 35 words, a
  run of same-length sentences, a dash in every paragraph: each is a prompt to
  reread, not a failure by count. Do not write toward a readability score. The
  half of one that works is sentence length; the other half rewards a short
  vague word over a precise technical one.

## Size

- **Size the document to its reader.** Before writing anything durable (a
  spec, a definition, a design record, a brief) name who reads it and what
  they must decide. Cut what does not serve that decision; of what stays, give
  the most room to what costs the reader most to get wrong. Being precise is
  not a reason to be long.
- **State the current conclusion, not the path to it.** Keep a failed query,
  corrected count or abandoned option only when it changes what the reader can
  trust, explains why the evidence cannot support a claim, or prevents the same
  mistake. State its effect, not its chronology.

## AI-isms

These are ready-made phrases that do your thinking for you. Reach for the
specific thing instead.

- "It's not just X, it's Y"; "not merely… but". The trap is the inflating
  contrast, where Y restates X more grandly. A contrast between two different
  things ("plain is not flat") is fine.
- "Here's the thing"; "Let me be clear"; "To be clear"; "It's worth noting
  that".
- Narrating your own rhetoric: "to put my conclusion first", "I want to be
  unfashionable here".
- The rule of three for rhythm, where the third item adds nothing.
- One-line paragraphs dropped in for drama; a dash fusing two thoughts that
  should be two sentences.
- Hollow openers and closers: "In today's world…", "In recent years…",
  "Ultimately…"; a final sentence that restates what you already said.
- "And that matters" as a transition; "at the end of the day".
- Announcing plainness instead of being plain: "read plainly", "in plain
  terms", "stripped of the framing", "read without euphemism". Cut the preface
  and state the thing.
- An abstract noun plus "is real": "the pressure behind it is real". Name the
  specific thing or show it.
- The evenly weighted verdict list, every item handed a matching
  adjective-noun stamp ("a real waste… a fair worry… a fair concern"). Real
  prose weights its items unequally.
- Unmasking language: "strip it down", "smuggled in", "no use pretending
  otherwise". It casts the other side as concealing something. State the
  point and skip the performance of exposing it.

A read-through hides repetition; a count finds it. On anything long, tally the
word you have leaned on, a punctuation tic, and sentence openers that repeat.

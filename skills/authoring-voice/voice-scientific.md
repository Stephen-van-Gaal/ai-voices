# Voice: scientific / grant & protocol

How to write scientific work — grant applications, study protocols, and the
background/significance, methods, ethics, and feasibility prose that fills them,
in health-services and quality-improvement research. Paste the text below the
rule as LLM context, or use it as a self-edit checklist. Guidelines, not rules.

---

North star: report to be understood and believed, not to impress or to win by
force. Your reader is a reviewer or a fellow clinician deciding whether to trust
the work and fund or act on it. Give them claims they can check, methods they
could repeat, and a clear line between what you found and what you infer. Plain
language still wins; precision wins more.

Shared craft. One idea per sentence. Make the actor the subject
and the action the verb — "we recruited participants," not "participants were
recruited" — and prefer active voice, dropping to passive only when the actor is
unknown or beside the point. Chain sentences: start with what the reader already
knows, end with what's new. Plain word over elevated ("use," not "utilize"),
verb over abstract noun, and cut every word the sentence survives without. Short
paragraphs, one point each; headers are plain labels.

Precision and consistency. Scientific writing does not reward
elegant variation. Call the same thing the same name every time: a *participant*
is not later a *subject* or a *respondent*; a *consult note* is not a *referral
letter* three lines on. Pick one term per concept and keep it, even when the
repetition feels heavy — the reader must never wonder whether two words mean the
same thing. Consistency outranks rhythm — don't reach for a
synonym just to vary the wording. Define each technical term at first use; expand an acronym once,
then use it. Name the specific construct, instrument, population, and time point
rather than gesturing at them.

Claims and evidence. Every empirical claim carries its support — a citation, a
number, or a named source — in the same sentence or the next. Keep what the data
show apart from what you infer: "X is associated with Y" is not "X causes Y";
"suggests" and "is consistent with" are not "demonstrates" or "proves."
Calibrate hedging to the evidence: hedge what is genuinely uncertain, state
plainly what is established, and don't hedge a settled point to sound careful.
Avoid both directions of failure — overclaim (*proves, confirms, novel, first,
optimal*) and a fog of reflexive hedging (*may potentially suggest a possible*).
Report magnitudes and effect sizes, not just direction; reserve "significant"
for its statistical sense or avoid the word. Distinguish your own contribution
from prior work, and don't claim more than the design can deliver. Cite by a
precise, repeatable method: search on a strategy you record, extract each claim
with the source it came from, check every claim you attribute against the source
text rather than its abstract, and verify each reference field by field against
the record it names.

Quantify. A number beats an adjective — "54% of progress-note content," not "much
of the note." Give units, the denominator, and the uncertainty you have (n, %,
range or confidence interval). State the comparator: better, longer, more common
than what? Don't dress an estimate as a measurement — mark a planning assumption
as an assumption, and a projection as a projection.

Structure and reporting. Write to the shape the reader expects, so they can find
and judge each part: for a grant, significance, objectives, methods, feasibility,
ethics; for a protocol, the reporting standard for the design (SRQR, COREQ, and
the like). Lead each section and each paragraph with its point, then support it.
Make methods reproducible — name the design, the population and sampling, the
instruments and their versions, the procedure, and the analysis, so another team
could run the study from the text. Each fact has one home; cross-reference it
rather than restate it, so the reader never re-reads the same content as the
document moves on.

Register and objectivity. Keep the tone neutral and measured, and let the
evidence carry the weight. "We" is fine, and usually clearer, for what the team
did; keep the focus on the work, not the authors. The significance section may
argue and motivate — open it on the size of the problem, shown with evidence —
but methods and results stay maximally plain and report rather than persuade.
Don't editorialize a result; state it and let it stand. Tense, lightly: past for
what was done and found, present for established knowledge and for what the
document itself does ("this protocol sets out…").

Narrative vignettes (patient stories, case examples). A vignette is evidence in
narrative form. The reader must finish it believing it happened, which they will
not do if the narrator is visibly steering. It usually sits in the significance
section, which may argue; a vignette argues by reporting, so it keeps the
plainness of methods rather than the licence of the section around it. Report the
case, and let one sentence at the end say what it means. That sentence is the
whole of the licence; nothing before it explains.

Write in the past tense. The historic present is a literary device, and it turns
a case report into a short story. Write the fact, then stop, giving each sentence
one clause. A flourish arrives as a subordinate clause — the aside after a dash,
the participle trailing the fact — so a sentence built from a main clause alone
has nowhere to put one. Where a second thought is worth having, it gets its own
sentence. Say what happened, not what it means: the meaning is the reader's to
make until the closing sentence, and the details carry it without help, since
page four of a five-page note needs no gloss. Reach for the specific noun — a
number, a page, a date, a measurement. "Six weeks of breathlessness" over "weeks
of breathlessness," "an 8 mm nodule in the right upper lobe" over "a small spot
on the lung," wherever the reader can carry the term. End the narrative on the
last fact and let the interpretive sentence follow it, because a verdict reached
early is the most reliable tell there is: the facts have already made the point,
and saying it as they go tells the reader you did not trust them to get there.
Three counts catch what the drafting rules miss — dashes above about one per
paragraph, any sentence past 35 words, and any sentence inside the narrative that
could be deleted without losing a fact. Each is usually interpretation that
arrived without being asked for.

Positioning against prior work. Locate the study against a real gap, stated
fairly. Characterize earlier instruments and studies accurately, credit what
they got right, then name the specific limitation your work addresses. Argue with
the evidence and the reasoning, never the people. Don't strawman the field, and
don't claim a novelty you can't support — "needs-grounded rather than
expert-nominated" is a defensible distinction; "the first study ever to…" rarely
is.

Avoid these tells (scientific, plus the generic AI-isms that still apply):
- Overclaim verbs and superlatives: *proves, confirms, establishes,
  demonstrates* (for associational data); *novel, unprecedented, first, optimal,
  gold-standard, paradigm* — unless literally true and shown.
- *Clearly, obviously, it is well known that, of course* — either cite it or cut
  it. The certainty is doing the work a citation should.
- Vague quantifiers standing in for evidence: *very, substantial, considerable,
  numerous, a growing body of* — give the number or the source.
- *Significant* used to mean "important" — it collides with the statistical
  sense; say "large," "meaningful," or give the figure.
- Anthropomorphism: "the data believe," "the study wants," "the model thinks" —
  the team does things; the data show things.
- Over-hedging stacks: *may potentially possibly* — one calibrated hedge, not
  three.
- Ready-made phrasings that do your thinking for you: "it's not just X, it's Y"; "here's the thing"; the rule of
  three for rhythm; hollow openers and closers ("In recent years…",
  "Ultimately…"); announcing plainness instead of being plain; the
  evenly-weighted verdict list with matching adjective-noun stamps.

A read-through hides inconsistent terminology and silent repetition; a count
finds both. On anything long, scan for two words used for one concept, a claim
that arrived without a citation, a number without its denominator or comparator,
and a section that repeats what another section owns.

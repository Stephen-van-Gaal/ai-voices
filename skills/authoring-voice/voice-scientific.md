# Voice: scientific

How to sound in scientific work: grant applications, study protocols, and the
background, significance, methods, ethics and feasibility prose that fills
them, in health-services and quality-improvement research. What a grant or
protocol must carry, and in what order, is `document-types-scientific.md`.

`voice-base.md` holds the rules on words, sentences, readability and AI-isms.
This guide adds what a reviewer needs: claims they can check, methods they
could repeat, and a clear line between what you found and what you infer.
Plain language still wins; precision wins more.

These are guidelines rather than rules: where a particular word or
construction reads more clearly to a reviewer, use it.

## Precision and consistency

- **Call the same thing the same name every time.** A *participant* is not
  later a *subject* or a *respondent*; a *consult note* is not a *referral
  letter* three lines on. Scientific writing does not reward elegant
  variation, and the reader must never wonder whether two words mean the same
  thing. Consistency outranks rhythm.
- **Define each technical term at first use.** Expand an acronym once, then
  use it.
- **Name the specific construct, instrument, population and time point.** Do
  not gesture at them.
- **"We" is fine for what the team did**, and usually clearer. Keep the focus
  on the work, not the authors.

## Claims and evidence

- **Every empirical claim carries its support** (a citation, a number or a
  named source) in the same sentence or the next.
- **Keep what the data show apart from what you infer.** "X is associated with
  Y" is not "X causes Y". "Suggests" and "is consistent with" are not
  "demonstrates" or "proves".
- **Calibrate hedging to the evidence.** Hedge what is uncertain, state plainly
  what is established, and do not hedge a settled point to sound careful. Both
  directions fail: overclaim (*proves, confirms, novel, first, optimal*) and a
  fog of reflexive hedging (*may potentially suggest a possible*).
- **Report magnitudes, not just direction.** Reserve *significant* for its
  statistical sense, or avoid the word.
- **Do not claim more than the design can deliver.** Distinguish your own
  contribution from prior work in the sentence that claims it.
- **Cite by a method you could repeat.** Search on a strategy you record,
  extract each claim with the source it came from, check every claim you
  attribute against the source text rather than its abstract, and verify each
  reference field by field against the record it names.

## Quantify

- **A number beats an adjective.** "54% of progress-note content", not "much
  of the note".
- **Give units, the denominator, and the uncertainty you have** (n, %, range
  or confidence interval).
- **State the comparator.** Better, longer, more common than what?
- **Do not dress an estimate as a measurement.** Mark a planning assumption as
  an assumption and a projection as a projection.

## Register and tense

- **Keep the tone neutral and let the evidence carry the weight.** The
  significance section may argue and motivate; methods and results report
  rather than persuade. Do not editorialize a result. State it and let it
  stand.
- **Argue with the evidence and the reasoning, never the people.** Do not
  strawman the field.
- **Tense, lightly.** Past for what was done and found, present for
  established knowledge and for what the document itself does ("this protocol
  sets out…").

## Narrative vignettes

A patient story or case example is evidence in narrative form. The reader must
finish it believing it happened, which they will not do if the narrator is
visibly steering. It usually sits in the significance section, which may
argue; a vignette argues by reporting, so it keeps the plainness of methods
rather than the licence of the section around it.

- **Report the case, and let one sentence at the end say what it means.** That
  sentence is the whole of the licence; nothing before it explains. A verdict
  reached early is the most reliable tell there is: the facts have already
  made the point, and saying it as they go tells the reader you did not trust
  them to get there.
- **Write in the past tense.** The historic present is a literary device, and
  it turns a case report into a short story.
- **Give each sentence one clause.** A flourish arrives as a subordinate
  clause, the aside after a dash or the participle trailing the fact, so a
  sentence built from a main clause alone has nowhere to put one. A second
  thought worth having gets its own sentence.
- **Say what happened, not what it means.** The details carry it without help;
  page four of a five-page note needs no gloss.
- **Reach for the specific noun.** A number, a page, a date, a measurement.
  "Six weeks of breathlessness" over "weeks of breathlessness"; "an 8 mm nodule
  in the right upper lobe" over "a small spot on the lung", wherever the reader
  can carry the term.
- **End the narrative on the last fact** and let the interpretive sentence
  follow it.

Three counts catch what the drafting rules miss: dashes above about one per
paragraph, any sentence past 35 words, and any sentence inside the narrative
that could be deleted without losing a fact. Each is usually interpretation
that arrived without being asked for.

## Tells to avoid

The AI-isms in `voice-base.md` still apply. These are the scientific ones.

- Overclaim verbs and superlatives: *proves, confirms, establishes,
  demonstrates* for associational data; *novel, unprecedented, first, optimal,
  gold-standard, paradigm*, unless literally true and shown.
- *Clearly, obviously, it is well known that, of course.* Either cite it or cut
  it. The certainty is doing the work a citation should.
- Vague quantifiers standing in for evidence: *numerous, a growing body of*.
  Give the number or the source.
- *Significant* used to mean "important". It collides with the statistical
  sense; say "large", "meaningful", or give the figure.
- Anthropomorphism: "the data believe", "the study wants", "the model thinks".
  The team does things; the data show things.
- Over-hedging stacks: *may potentially possibly*. One calibrated hedge.

A read-through hides inconsistent terminology; a count finds it. On anything
long, scan for two words used for one concept, a claim that arrived without a
citation, and a number without its denominator or comparator.

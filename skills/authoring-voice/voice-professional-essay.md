# Voice: professional / essay

How to write professional prose and essays. Paste the text below the rule as LLM
context, or use it as a self-edit checklist. Guidelines, not rules.

---

North star: write to be understood, not to impress. Approachable, plain
language. Aim for the meaning first and then find the words for it — don't reach
for a ready-made phrase and let it do your thinking. These are guidelines, not
rules; if a particular word or device is the clearest way to make the point, use
it.

Sentences. One idea per sentence. Default to short and medium; vary the rhythm
but cap the long ones. If a sentence carries more than one subordinate clause,
or needs a second read, split it. Make the actor the subject and the action the
verb — say who does what, not "there was a failure of" or "a decision was
reached." Prefer active voice; use passive only when the actor is unknown or
beside the point. Put the words that carry the point at the end, where they land
— end on the point, not on a cadence. A run of same-length medium sentences is
itself a tell of generated prose; the cheapest fix is to plant one short blunt
sentence and let it break the rhythm.

Flow. Connect sentences: start with something the reader already knows and end
with what's new. A paragraph should read as a chain, each sentence picking up
where the last left off — not a row of separate facts.

Paragraphs and mechanics. Short paragraphs, one idea each. Lists only for real
enumeration — never to chop up an argument. Headers are plain labels, not
teasers. En dashes sparingly; when a dash is doing the work of a period, use the
period. Watch the dash-aside ( – like this – ) turning into the default sentence
shape: if a count turns up dozens, the rhythm has gone monotone and half should
be commas or full stops.

Words. Plain over elevated — "use," not "utilize"; "before," not "prior to."
Prefer the everyday word to the technical one when it carries the same meaning;
keep the technical term only when it is the precise one. Concrete over abstract.
Prefer a verb to an abstract noun — "vacates a space," not "the vacating of a
space." Cut every word that earns nothing: if the sentence survives without it,
drop it. Cut intensifiers and emphasis-fillers: very, deeply, fundamentally, simply,
genuinely, profoundly — and real, actual, true when they only assert weight
("a real choice," "the actual disease"). Name the thing instead. No metaphor for decoration, and none you've seen in print a hundred
times — a dead metaphor ("toe the line," "low-hanging fruit") is a long way of
saying a plain thing. Keep the few live ones that are the clearest way to say it
(launchpad, flywheel) and retire the rest.

Stance. State claims directly and own them. Be precise: a vague word often hides
a decision you haven't made — name the specific thing rather than gesturing at
it. Calibrate confidence to evidence — assert
what you know, mark what is hypothesis, sit honestly with what isn't settled,
and don't hedge what is certain. Avoid the tells of arrogance: don't call a
contested claim obvious or simple, don't tell readers what they think, don't
lecture.

Disagreeing well. Argue with the reasoning, never the people; drop sarcasm,
contempt, and point-scoring. Make a criticism once — repeating that the other
side never argued it, never stated it, never defended it reads as nagging, and
scores points instead of making them. Prefer the systemic account to the
personal one: "no one chose this on purpose" beats "they chose it and hid it" —
calmer, and usually truer. Drop unmasking language — "strip it down," "smuggled
in," "read without euphemism," "no use pretending otherwise" — which casts the
other side as concealing something; state the point and skip the performance of
exposing it. Concede real points early and plainly. The opening sets the frame
for the whole piece, so when you risk reading as arrogant or self-interested,
lower the temperature without going limp: attribute the diagnosis to a source
the reader already trusts, grant the rival position as one a reasonable person
could hold, and save your one non-negotiable for the end.

Structure and persuasion. Persuade by order and evidence, not by volume. Where
it fits, open on a concrete particular — a case, a number, a specific moment —
and let the general point grow out of it. One claim, its support, its
consequence, then stop. State the point once and show the evidence; trust the
reader to draw it, and don't re-chew the verdict. Don't stack three reasons
where one strong reason carries the weight. Use examples and numbers in place of
adjectives. Name the strongest objection and answer it in a sentence. Plain is
not flat — the force comes from clear logic and honest concession.

Avoid these AI-isms (they're ready-made phrases that do your thinking for you —
reach for the specific thing instead):
- "It's not just X, it's Y"; "not merely… but." (The trap is the inflating
  contrast, where Y just restates X more grandly. Contrast between two genuinely
  different things — "plain is not flat" — is fine.)
- "Here's the thing"; "Let me be clear"; "To be clear"; "It's worth noting that."
- Narrating your own rhetoric: "to put my conclusion first," "I want to be
  unfashionable here."
- The rule of three for rhythm — "better selected, better scoped, better funded"
  — when the third item adds nothing.
- One-line paragraphs dropped in for drama; em dashes fusing two thoughts that
  should be two sentences.
- Hollow openers and closers: "In today's world…"; "Ultimately…"; a final
  sentence that just restates what you already said.
- "and that matters" as a transition; "at the end of the day."
- Announcing plainness instead of being plain: "Read plainly,"; "in plain
  terms"; "stripped of the framing"; "read without euphemism." Cut the preface
  and state the thing.
- "The pressure behind it is real"; "the worry is real." An abstract noun plus
  "is real," asserting weight instead of naming the specific thing or showing
  it. Name it.
- The evenly-weighted verdict list — every item handed a matching adjective-noun
  stamp, "a real waste… a fair worry… a fair concern." The symmetry is the tell;
  real prose weights its items unequally.

A read-through hides repetition; a count finds it. On anything long, scan for the
word you've leaned on, a single punctuation tic, and sentence openers that
repeat — invisible in flow, obvious in a tally.
